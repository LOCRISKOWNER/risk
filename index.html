What this page shows

Some researchers expect AI systems to start improving themselves soon, with each generation helping to build the next. If that happens, a key question is whether the resulting systems would pursue goals that differ from what their developers intended, and how likely that is to end in catastrophe. This model breaks the question into a chain of steps. Each step has a probability you can change, and the page multiplies them together.

Key terms
RSI (recursive self-improvement)
AI systems doing much of the research that makes AI better, so progress feeds on itself.
Superintelligence
AI far more capable than humans at almost every intellectual task.
Fast and slow takeoff
Whether RSI leads to superintelligence within months (fast) or over years (slow). Fast takeoff leaves less time to notice and fix problems.
Misalignment
An AI developing goals that differ from what its developers intended, and acting to protect them from correction.
Scalable oversight
Methods that let humans, helped by AI, supervise and correct systems too capable to check directly.
Catastrophe
A misaligned AI causing at least a million deaths or comparable global harm. The strict definition counts only permanent loss of human control, or at least 10% of people killed.
How to use it
  1. The large number at the top is the model's overall estimate of the chance of catastrophe by 2031, including the chance that RSI starts in the next two years at all. The figure beside it assumes RSI does start.
  2. Choose a preset (Optimist, Central or Pessimist), or move any slider to set your own view.
  3. The sections below show where the risk drops away along the chain, which inputs matter most, and how uncertain the result is.

Treat the numbers as illustrative. The default values are one person's judgement calls, not forecasts derived from data. Published expert estimates of AI catastrophe range from well under 1% to above 50%, depending on who is asked and how the question is defined. The model covers misalignment only, not misuse of AI by people or conflict between countries.

Speed of takeoff

How quickly RSI leads to superintelligence. In the fast branch it takes months, leaving little time to notice and fix problems. The rest of the probability goes to the slow branch, where it takes years. The first slider only affects the overall figure, not the figure assuming RSI.

Fast takeoff: misalignment and oversight

Whether advanced systems end up misaligned, and how much oversight techniques reduce that. Oversight is a relative cut: at 20%, a 50% chance of misalignment becomes 40%, not 30%.

Fast takeoff: from misalignment to catastrophe

A misaligned system that is not corrected still has to pursue a catastrophic outcome, succeed against human responses, and do so before the end of 2031. Each is a separate judgement.

Slow takeoff

Worlds where RSI takes years, not months, to produce superintelligence. They have their own inputs because more time changes how well oversight works, how people respond, and whether harm lands before 2031. Untick the box to see the fast-takeoff figure on its own.

Which inputs matter most

Each bar shows how the total changes when that one input is moved 15 percentage points down and up from its current setting, with everything else held fixed. A longer bar means the result is more sensitive to that input. The black line marks the current total. "Overall" includes the chance that RSI happens at all.

How uncertain is the result?

The sliders give one best guess for each input, but every one of them is uncertain. This simulation (a Monte Carlo analysis) treats each slider as the middle of a range of possible values, picks 10,000 random combinations, runs the model on each, and shows how widely the answers spread.

Share of each input's variation that moves together: when alignment turns out hard, misalignment, pursuit, success and timing all tend to be high and oversight low. Zero treats every input as independent.

P(> 5%) is the share of simulations in which the chance of catastrophe comes out above 5%. The 80% range runs from the 10th to the 90th percentile of results.

What drives the spread

Each bar shows how closely an input's random values track the result. Right means higher values push the result up; left means they push it down. Inputs that tend to move together through the shared "hard world" factor all look important when that factor is high. Set it to zero to see each input on its own.

How the model works
fast = P(super in months) × misaligned × (1 − oversight)
       × pursues × succeeds × by 2031
slow = (1 − P(super in months)) × misaligned_slow × (1 − oversight_slow)
       × pursues_slow × succeeds_slow × by 2031_slow

assuming RSI  = fast + slow
overall       = P(RSI in next 2 years) × assuming RSI
strict        = assuming RSI × strict share

The model covers misalignment only. It leaves out misuse of AI by people and conflict between countries, which would add to the total. The fast and slow branches cannot both happen, so their risks add without overlap.

The default values are judgement calls, not forecasts derived from data. Use the presets and sliders to see what you would need to believe to reach a different answer.

The uncertainty section draws each input from a logit-normal distribution centred on its slider, which keeps values between 0% and 100%, with a shared factor that moves the inputs together. The spread and the shared factor are also judgement calls, so the intervals show how the model's assumptions combine, not a calibrated forecast.
